# Product Field — Aspect Definitions

The twelve aspects of the Product Field, organized by corner. Each aspect includes what it covers, where it sits on the canvas, what to think about when exploring it, guiding questions for mapping sessions, and common misplacements to watch for during audits.

For how the aspects connect to each other, see `relations.md`. For the overall model, see `model.md`.

---

## IDEA Corner (inside + purpose)

### GOALS

**What it is:** The strategic or business objectives the organization wants to achieve with this product. Why the organization is investing in this innovation. Making goals explicit gives direction, makes impact verifiable, and surfaces conflicting objectives early.

**Position:** Inside + Purpose (top-left of context)

**Think about:** growth, protection, reputation, change, sustainability, market position, revenue targets, strategic bets

**Guiding questions:**
- Why is the organization investing in this product?
- What does success look like for the business — not for users, but for the organization itself?
- Are there multiple goals? Do they reinforce each other or compete?
- What happens if this product fails? What's at stake?
- How does this product fit the organization's broader strategy?
- Is this a growth bet, a defensive move, or something else?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- User goals → these belong in MOTIVATIONS (goals are organizational, motivations are user-side)
- Product features or roadmap items → these belong in solution
- Revenue projections → if these are targets, they're GOALS; if they're market sizing, they're CUSTOMERS

---

### DRIVERS

**What it is:** The people and teams accountable for the product's outcome. They steer the innovation process and make decisions in pursuit of the organization's goals. Understanding who they are, what power they have, and what they're capable of helps clarify decision-making and spot leadership gaps.

**Position:** Inside, between purpose and implementation (left side of context)

**Think about:** personality, competences, qualities, roles, decision-making powers, mandate, commitment, alignment

**Guiding questions:**
- Who are the people or teams driving this product forward?
- Do they have the authority to make the decisions this product needs?
- What skills and experience do they bring? What are they missing?
- Are the drivers aligned on what this product should be, or are there competing visions?
- Is there a single accountable person, or is ownership diffuse?
- How committed are the drivers — is this their main focus or a side project?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- End users of the product → these belong in USERS (drivers are internal, users are external)
- The organization as a whole → be specific about which people and teams
- External partners or contractors → if they're building, they may be ENABLERS; if they're deciding, they may be DRIVERS

---

### uniqueness

**What it is:** What differentiates this product from alternatives. What compels people to choose it instead of what they use today. A clear uniqueness is the core of the value proposition's strength and a key predictor of adoption.

**Position:** Inside + Purpose (top-left of core)

**Think about:** quality, performance, pricing, image, UX, brand, novel approach, combination of features, speed, simplicity

**Guiding questions:**
- What makes this product different from what already exists?
- Why would someone switch from their current solution to this?
- Is the uniqueness something users actually care about, or is it only interesting to the team?
- Can this uniqueness be easily copied? How defensible is it?
- Is it one killer differentiator or a combination of things?
- How would you explain the uniqueness in one sentence to a potential user?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Features of the solution → uniqueness is about differentiation, not functionality. "It has a dashboard" is solution; "it's the only one that shows real-time competitive pricing" is uniqueness
- Marketing claims → uniqueness should be grounded in something real, not aspirational messaging
- Internal capabilities → "we have a great team" is ENABLERS or DRIVERS, not uniqueness

## VALUE Corner (outside + purpose)

### USERS

**What it is:** The people who actually use the product to overcome a problem while pursuing their motivations. Understanding who they are, what sets them apart, and how they differ from each other helps target the right problem and design the right solution.

**Position:** Outside + Purpose (top-right of context)

**Think about:** identity, relationships, actions, expertise, interests, segments, behaviors, context of use

**Guiding questions:**
- Who will actually use this product? Be specific — not "everyone" but which people in which situations.
- What are they like? What do they know? What's their daily reality?
- Are there distinct user segments with different needs?
- How do they currently deal with the problem — what's their workaround?
- Where and when would they use this product?
- What would make them stop using it?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Customers who don't use the product → these belong in CUSTOMERS (the person who pays is not always the person who uses)
- Demographic descriptions without behavioral context → users are defined by what they do and need, not just who they are
- Internal users/stakeholders → if they're inside the organization, they're likely DRIVERS or ENABLERS

---

### MOTIVATIONS

**What it is:** The needs, desires, and goals that drive users. What they're trying to achieve in their lives or work that this product could help with. Understanding motivations provides a compass for design, a basis for measuring impact, and a connection to the problem.

**Position:** Outside + Purpose (top of context, between GOALS and USERS)

**Think about:** pleasure, productivity, security, respect, prosperity, status, convenience, autonomy, belonging

**Guiding questions:**
- What are users trying to achieve in their lives or work?
- What gains are they looking for? What pains are they trying to avoid?
- Which of these motivations are strong enough to drive a change in behavior?
- Are the motivations the same across user segments, or do they differ?
- What would the ideal outcome look like from the user's perspective?
- How do these motivations connect to the problem this product addresses?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Organizational goals → these belong in GOALS (motivations are user-side, goals are organization-side)
- Problems → motivations are what users want to achieve; problems are what gets in the way. "I want to grow my practice" is a motivation; "I can't find reliable product data" is a problem
- Feature requests → "I want a mobile app" is not a motivation, it's a proposed solution

---

### problem

**What it is:** What gets in the way of users pursuing their motivations. The obstacle, pain, or unmet need that the product addresses. A well-understood problem is the foundation of the entire value proposition — it's the reason the product exists.

**Position:** Outside + Purpose (top-right of core)

**Think about:** fears, pains, lacks, costs, roadblocks, frustrations, inefficiencies, risks

**Guiding questions:**
- What specific problem do users face when pursuing their motivations?
- How painful is this problem? Is it a minor annoyance or a serious blocker?
- How frequently do they encounter it?
- What does the problem cost them — in time, money, stress, missed opportunities?
- Is this a problem they're aware of, or one they've accepted as normal?
- Who else is affected by this problem besides the direct users?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- The absence of your product → "they don't have a tool for X" is not a problem, it's a gap. The problem is what happens because they don't have it
- Technical challenges → "our API is slow" is a PRODUCTION or solution issue, not a user problem
- Organizational problems → "we're not growing fast enough" is GOALS, not problem

## RESOURCES Corner (inside + implementation)

### ENABLERS

**What it is:** The resources, assets, and capabilities that make the product innovation possible in the first place. What the organization can draw on. Understanding what's available and what's missing helps assess feasibility and spot risks early.

**Position:** Inside + Implementation (bottom-left of context)

**Think about:** know-how, technology, infrastructure, culture, rights, IP, data, partnerships, funding, talent

**Guiding questions:**
- What resources and capabilities does the organization already have that this product can build on?
- What's missing? What needs to be acquired or built before the product can be realized?
- Are there unique assets — data, technology, relationships — that give an advantage?
- Are there constraints — regulatory, technical, financial — that limit what's possible?
- What enabling capabilities exist in the broader ecosystem (partners, platforms, open source)?
- How hard would it be for a competitor to assemble the same enablers?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Production processes → "our CI/CD pipeline" is PRODUCTION, not ENABLERS. Enablers are the raw capabilities; production is how they're organized into processes
- Team members → specific people driving decisions belong in DRIVERS. Enablers are capabilities, not individuals
- Distribution assets → "our sales team" could be ENABLERS (capability) or DISTRIBUTION (channel) — ask whether it's about what we *can* do or how we *reach* customers

---

### PRODUCTION

**What it is:** How the solution gets built. The activities, processes, systems, and structures that turn enablers into a working product. Understanding production clarifies what's needed to build and maintain the solution, and what it costs.

**Position:** Inside + Implementation (bottom-left of context)

**Think about:** activities, know-how, process, facilities, material, team structure, cost structure, cycle time, quality assurance

**Guiding questions:**
- What activities are needed to build this product?
- What does the production process look like — is it software development, manufacturing, service delivery, or a mix?
- What skills, tools, and processes are needed?
- Are the right production capabilities in place, or do they need to be built?
- What does the cost structure look like?
- What are the key risks in production — bottlenecks, dependencies, single points of failure?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Distribution activities → "shipping the product" or "running marketing campaigns" belong in DISTRIBUTION
- The solution itself → production is how you build it; solution is what you build
- Enablers → "we have React expertise" is an ENABLER (capability); "we run two-week sprints" is PRODUCTION (process)

---

### solution

**What it is:** How the product tackles the users' problem. It removes obstacles, relieves pains, or opens up new possibilities. The solution can be a service, hardware, software, process, or any combination. Describing it clearly shapes what needs to be built and what production resources are required.

**Position:** Inside + Implementation (bottom-left of core)

**Think about:** service, hardware, software, rights, processes, components, architecture, scope

**Guiding questions:**
- How does this product solve the problem? What's the core mechanism?
- What type of product is it — software, service, physical product, platform, or hybrid?
- What are its key components?
- What does the user experience look like? How do they interact with the solution?
- What's in scope for the first version, and what's deliberately left out?
- How does the solution connect to the uniqueness? Does the way it solves the problem create differentiation?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Features framed as uniqueness → "it has offline mode" is solution; "it's the only product in the category that works offline" is uniqueness
- Production details → how you build it is PRODUCTION; what you build is solution
- The problem restated as a solution → "we solve the data problem" doesn't describe the solution — describe what the product actually does

## MARKET Corner (outside + implementation)

### DISTRIBUTION

**What it is:** How the product reaches customers. The channels, marketing, sales processes, logistics, and technology that get the product into the minds and hands of users and customers. Understanding distribution informs go-to-market strategy and customer acquisition cost.

**Position:** Outside + Implementation (bottom-right of context)

**Think about:** marketing, sales, logistics, channels, technology, partnerships, customer acquisition, conversion

**Guiding questions:**
- How will the product reach its customers? Through which channels?
- What does the go-to-market strategy look like?
- Is distribution direct, through partners, through platforms, or a mix?
- What does customer acquisition cost look like?
- What will it take to convert interested users or prospects into paying customers?
- Are there distribution advantages — existing channels, partnerships, network effects?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Production processes → "our development workflow" is PRODUCTION, not DISTRIBUTION
- The product itself → a product feature that aids distribution (like a referral system) is still solution; the channel it creates is DISTRIBUTION
- Trade shows, conferences → these are DISTRIBUTION (they reach customers), not PRODUCTION (they don't build the product)

---

### CUSTOMERS

**What it is:** The people or organizations who pay for the product. They may or may not be the same as users. Understanding customers — who they are, how they buy, what they're worth — is essential for business model viability and market sizing.

**Position:** Outside, between purpose and implementation (right side of context)

**Think about:** roles, industries, professions, sizes, addressability, buying behavior, lifetime value, budget

**Guiding questions:**
- Who will actually pay for this product? Is it the same person who uses it?
- How do they make buying decisions? Who's involved in the decision?
- What's their budget for this kind of solution?
- How large is the addressable customer base?
- How much potential revenue or lifetime value does each customer represent?
- What would make them choose to pay for this over the alternatives?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Users → if someone uses the product but doesn't pay, they're a USER, not a CUSTOMER. In B2B especially, the buyer and the user are often different people
- Market size → raw market numbers without a description of who the customers actually are — be specific about segments
- Distribution channels → "dental distributors" might be DISTRIBUTION (channel) or CUSTOMERS (buyers) depending on context

---

### alternatives

**What it is:** What people use today instead of this product. Anything that currently addresses or circumvents the problem — competing products, workarounds, manual processes, or even deliberate ignorance. Understanding alternatives sets a realistic benchmark and informs uniqueness.

**Position:** Outside + Implementation (bottom-right of core)

**Think about:** other products, workarounds, entrenched behavior, DIY solutions, ignorance, doing nothing, adjacent solutions

**Guiding questions:**
- What are people doing today to deal with the problem? What are they using?
- Which alternatives are most entrenched? What would it take to displace them?
- What do alternatives do well? Where do they fall short?
- Are there indirect alternatives — workarounds, spreadsheets, asking a colleague — that people don't even think of as "solutions"?
- What's the cost of switching from the current alternative to this product?
- Is "doing nothing" a real alternative? How common is it?

**Watch for misplacements:**
- Uniqueness → "our competitor doesn't have real-time data" describes their weakness, which supports your uniqueness — but the alternative itself is the competitor's product
- The problem → "there's no good solution" is a characterization of the problem space, not an alternative. Alternatives are what people actually do today
- Future competitors → focus on what exists now, not what might appear

## How to Use This File

**During mapping (`/pf:map`):** Use the guiding questions as starting points, not as a checklist. Follow the thread based on what the user reveals. The think-about themes help you explore breadth; the questions help you explore depth.

**During audit (`/pf:audit`):** Use the "watch for misplacements" to check whether facts are in the right area. When a fact seems misplaced, suggest where it might belong and ask the user to confirm.

**During import (`/pf:import`):** Use the definitions to classify incoming material. When content could fit multiple aspects, place it where it has the strongest fit and note the ambiguity.
